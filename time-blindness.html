Time blindness & ADHD

When you lose track of time, a reminder you can ignore is no reminder at all.

If you've surfaced from deep focus to find your meeting started ten minutes ago, not because you didn't care but because time slipped, Heads Up is built for exactly that.

Time blindness is real, and it's not a character flaw

Time blindness is difficulty sensing time passing: how much has gone by, how much is left. It's common with ADHD, and it doesn't respond to being told to "just pay attention." When you're absorbed in something, a calendar notification slides in and slides away, and the part of your brain that would have acted on it never got the message.

The usual advice (more alarms, more sticky notes, more calendars) adds more things that are just as easy to tune out. The problem was never that you needed another gentle nudge.

A reminder you can't tune out

  • It shows time passing. A ring in your menu bar fills as your next meeting gets closer, an outside clock for a sense of time that isn't always there inside. You glance and know, without doing the math.
  • It takes over the whole screen when the meeting starts. Not a banner in the corner you'll swipe away on reflex. Your entire screen, until you join or dismiss.
  • It can join for you. Not ready to switch yet? Press Auto-join and Heads Up opens the meeting the moment it starts. No second reminder to remember, no clock to watch.

It finds the Zoom, Meet, Teams, or Webex link in the invite, so joining is one click, with no scrambling through your calendar while you're already late. And you choose how much warning you get: fifteen minutes out, two, or right at the start.

Set it once, then stop managing it

There's no system to maintain, which is what quietly kills most reminder setups. You allow calendar access once, and it watches every calendar you already use (iCloud, Google, Outlook, Exchange). It skips all-day events, declined invites, and "free" blocks on its own, so it only speaks up for meetings that matter.
